Lodestone Battle Map Guide

Lodestone battle maps

How battle maps should look, the exact rules for anyone drawing tiles, the grid numbers to feed an image model, and tablet templates that line up with the Godot board. Built from your isometric reference. Every rule is a proposal until you mark it yours.

  • 2:1 dimetric
  • tile 128 × 64 px
  • 1 level = 32 px
  • lines at 26.565°
  • sun upper left
  • values 100 / 75 / 55
  • ink #373335
  • canvas 2400 × 1600

The look

A battle map is a small floating island on plain cream paper. It is built from chunky stone blocks stacked into terraces, each capped with a thick carpet of moss that spills over the edges. Water runs in channels and pours off ledges as short falls. A few ruins and round dark pines break the skyline. One soft sun from the upper left: tops brightest, left faces mid, right faces darkest. Warm dark outlines, muted sage, warm gray and teal; the only loud colours belong to the pieces and the UI.

What we keep from it

  • Big calm tops. The walkable surface is the lightest, quietest area; detail lives on cliffs and props.
  • Chunky height. Every step shows a dark cliff face, so height reads at a glance.
  • The island edge. The map ends in cliffs dropping into the paper with a few loose cubes falling away. No horizon.
  • Moss lips. The grass cap overhangs the stone and softens every block edge.
  • Few, big props. One hero structure, a handful of ruins and trees; most cells are plain ground.
With the Style Lab. The proposal takes the reference's structure (camera, blocks, moss, falls, light) and the Style Lab's surface (paper colour, riso grain over the frame, no black, stamped marks instead of rendering, region palettes for Earth to Alien). A battle then reads as a closer, tilted page of the same book as the world map. If you'd rather match the reference exactly, only rules B16 to B18 change.

The grid, in numbers

The world grid is square (1:1). Turn it 45° and look down from 30° with an orthographic camera, and each square becomes a diamond twice as wide as tall. Grid lines climb 1 pixel for every 2 across, which is 26.565°. This is the "pixel-art isometric" standard, and it is not true isometric (30° lines, a 1.73:1 diamond). Image models hear "isometric" as the true kind, so always say 2:1.

Rules for the art team

Numbers are art pixels. Art is drawn at 2× the engine (engine tile 64 × 32), so it stays crisp on big screens and the engine halves it at 720p.

Projection and grid

  • B12:1 dimetric. A tile is a diamond 128 wide, 64 tall. Grid lines at 26.565°.
  • B2Orthographic: no perspective, vanishing point, lens blur or tilt-shift. Parallel edges stay parallel across the map.
  • B3The world grid is square; only the camera makes it a diamond.
  • B4One cell, one tile, one piece. Nothing walkable straddles two cells.

Blocks and height

  • B5One height level is 32 px straight up. A full cube is 2 levels (64 px). Maps use heights 0 to 8.
  • B6Stone courses are 1 level tall with staggered joints, so a cliff's height can be counted.
  • B7Only two side faces ever show: LIT (facing lower-left) and SHADE (facing lower-right). Never draw back or bottom faces.
  • B8Side strips are cut from the top down to the neighbour's height. Put the moss lip and best detail at the top; the rest tiles vertically every 64 px.
  • B9The island hangs 2 levels below height 0, with a ragged bottom and 3 to 6 loose cubes falling below it.

Light and value

  • B10One sun, upper left, fixed on screen. It does not turn with the camera.
  • B11Every material: top 100%, lit face 75%, shade face 55%.
  • B12Shadows lean cool (toward #727877), lights lean warm (toward #d8cb97). Never pure gray.
  • B13No cast shadows painted into tiles. The engine adds contact shadows under pieces and props.

Line and edge

  • B14Outline #373335, never black: 3 px on the outer silhouette, 2 px on inner edges, 1 px joints and grass ticks in a darker tint of the face.
  • B15Block edges stay straight on the grid. Moss lips and props get the hand-cut wobble. Moss drapes 8 to 24 px over a top edge and is painted with the top sprite.

  • B16Flat fills plus at most one light band per face. Texture comes from marks one value step off the fill: grass fans, stone ticks, a few flowers or pebbles. No noise brushes or photo texture.
  • B17Home palette as above, measured from your reference.
  • B18The engine adds the riso grain over the finished frame. Don't paint grain into tiles.
  • B19No saturated blue or red in terrain or props; water is a muted teal. Blue and red belong to team rings, highlights and health bars.
  • B20Tops are the calmest, brightest surface so move and attack highlights read on them.

Water

  • B21Water tops are flat teal with two or three light streaks; the engine animates them.
  • B22Waterfalls are a 64 × 128 seamless vertical strip per face; the engine scrolls it and adds foam.

Props, set pieces and draw order

  • B23A prop stands on one cell: 128 × 256 canvas, feet on the pivot at (64, 224). Up to 192 px tall above the top.
  • B24Props that block sight look it: a ruin pillar about 2 levels tall, a tree about 1 level of canopy.
  • B25Big structures are drawn whole on the map template, then cut along the slice columns (every 64 px through grid corners). Each slice sorts with its cell.
  • B26Anything that can hang in front of a piece (arch tops, branches, banners) goes on the foreground occluder layer; the engine fades it when a piece is behind.
  • B27Tops read the same in all four camera turns (Q): no arrows, one-way slopes or text.
  • B28Props get a FRONT and a SIDE view; the engine mirrors them for the other two turns. Stairs need both.

Readability and files

  • B29Each terrain is recognisable by its top alone at engine size: grass, forest, water, rock, road, stone floor, stairs, ruin.
  • B30Pieces are the brightest, warmest thing on the map. Nothing taller than 1 level stands next to a deploy column.
  • B31Sprites go in game/art/source/battle/: g_top_1.png, stone_side_lit.png, stone_side_shade.png, pillar_front.png, pillar_side.png, fall_lit.png. Letters follow the map files: g grass, f forest, w water, r rock, d road, s stone, t stairs, u ruin.
  • B32Paint home colours only. Other regions come from the engine's palette map, so keep each material's three values distinct.

Drawing a map in Krita

  1. Open maps/<name>/<name>.psd (or .ora). Lock the CONTROL and GUIDE layers.
  2. Optional snapping: Grid and Guides docker, type Isometric, both angles 26.57, then nudge spacing until it sits on the template grid.
  3. Paint each pass on its own layer, bottom to top: cliffs and sides, tops, props, water and fx, foreground occluders. Keep those names; the import splits on them.
  4. Save. Claude slices and imports from the paint layers.

New map: build it in the combat sandbox editor (F1), save, and ask for its template; the script regenerates one per saved map.

Prompts for Krita AI Diffusion

A model can't hold a grid on its own, but it can when the template is its control. Add control-lineart.png as a Line Art control layer (strength 0.8 to 1.0) and control-depth.png as Depth (0.5 to 0.7). Put control-blocking.png or your rough paint on the canvas and Refine at 0.45 to 0.65; lower keeps the geometry. Then clip the result to the blocking silhouette and paint over seams by hand.

Recommendation on generating tiles. The art pipeline doc said never generate tile grids. I'd loosen that to: generate tiles only on top of these templates, with line art and depth as control, clipped to the masks and hand-fixed. Never ask for a tileset freehand; it won't hold 2:1, the pivots or the seams. Animation frames stay hand-made or code-made.

The zone pipeline

Maps are built in Godot. Krita makes the look. Every zone (forest, desert, dungeon, snow…) goes through three stages.

1. Prototype the zone in Krita

  1. Open art/templates/battle-maps/maps/sampler/sampler.ora. It's a test map built for this: every ground type, tall cliff and stone walls, a stepped stream, stairs, ruins, and open ground for key items.
  2. Hide CONTROL line art and the GUIDE layers. Import control-lineart.png and control-depth.png as hidden layers, and add them as Control Layers in the AI docker (Line Art 80%, Depth 55%).
  3. Pick a starting picture: import control-blocking-desert.png, -dungeon, -forest or -snow and hide the original blocking. No brushing needed. (Scribbling your own colours on top works too.)
  4. Prompt the zone (for example "desert mesa, sandstone cliffs, dry shrubs, campfire") and click Refine at 60–85%. Higher strength follows the prompt more; the control layers keep the grid either way. Try a few, keep the best, change the prompt, try again.
  5. Paint or refine the key items (campfire, fallen tree, ruins) on paint 3 props.
On the MacBook: if Refine fails with "MPSGraph does not support tensor dims larger than INT_MAX", open the docker's gear, Performance, and set Resolution multiplier to 0.5 (0.4 if it still fails). The full canvas is too big for one pass on MPS.

2. Break it out

  1. Hide paint 3 props and the CONTROL/GUIDE layers, then File > Export the whole canvas as art/paintings/sampler-desert.png.
  2. Show only paint 3 props and export it as art/paintings/sampler-desert-props.png (transparent, full canvas).
  3. Tell me "concept in", or run it yourself:
    python3 tools/art/break_out.py art/paintings/sampler-desert.png --map sampler --set desert --props art/paintings/sampler-desert-props.png
  4. It writes every top texture, side strip and prop into game/art/source/battle/desert/, plus sampler-desert-breakout.png showing each piece and where it was cut.

3. Clean up and play

Open the cut pieces in Krita and tidy them (steps below): make textures seamless, paint the trees out of top_forest, rename props to the game's names. Then pick tile set: desert in the sandbox.

Making or cleaning tiles in Krita

This assumes Krita AI Diffusion with the Local Managed Server (SDXL, ZavyChroma XL) on the MacBook and the repo cloned. Button names match plugin 1.5x.

A folder of PNGs with fixed names. Make any of them; whatever you skip falls back to the current mossy art.

Sets live in game/art/source/battle/<set>/, for example game/art/source/battle/snow/top_grass.png. A new folder is a new set. A dungeon is mostly top_stone, side_masonry and prop_pillar; a forest set is mostly the grass, forest and pine files.

1. One-time setup

  1. In the AI Image Generation docker, click the gear, then Styles, then +. Name it Lodestone battle, model ZavyChroma XL, no LoRA.
  2. Style prompt: {prompt}, flat illustrated 2D game art, flat fills with small hand-drawn marks, warm dark outlines, muted riso print colours, clean readable shapes. Negative: the Negative block in the prompts section above.
  3. Pick Lodestone battle in the docker's style dropdown.

2. A ground or wall texture

  1. File > New, 1024 × 1024, any background.
  2. Leave Strength at 100% (the button says Generate). There's nothing to keep, so you want a fresh picture.
  3. Paste the texture prompt for that file (texture prompts are in the Picsart section below; they work in Krita too) and change the subject line, for example "snow-covered ground with soft drifts and a few footprints". Click Generate a few times and click the best thumbnail, then Apply.
  4. Make it seamless: press W to turn on wrap-around mode. Now you see the picture repeated, and the seams show as hard lines. Run Layer > Transform > Offset (Shift+O) by 512 × 512 to move the seams into the middle as a cross. Select a band over the cross with the rectangle tool, set Strength to 40%, and click Refine with the same prompt. Repeat if a seam still shows, or paint it out by hand.
  5. For a side texture, check you have 4 even rows of stone. If not, generate again; the game lines each row up with a height level.
  6. Flatten, then File > Export as PNG to game/art/source/battle/<set>/<file>.png, for example snow/top_grass.png.

3. A prop

  1. File > New, 832 × 1216 for tall things (pines, pillars) or 1024 × 1024 for the rest, with a cream background (#eae8d6).
  2. Strength 100%, paste the prop prompt from the Picsart section (change the subject, for example "heavy snow on each tier"), Generate, Apply the best.
  3. Cut it out: Select > Select from Colour Range on the cream (or the magic wand), press Delete, then crop so the feet sit on the bottom edge, centred left to right.
  4. Export as PNG with transparency to game/art/source/battle/<set>/prop_pine_1.png and so on.

4. See it in the game

  1. Open the Godot project once so it imports the new PNGs.
  2. Run the combat sandbox, open or build a map, and pick tile set: snow in the right panel. Save the map to keep that choice.
  3. Commit and push, or tell me "new art in" and I'll check colours and sizes against the rules.

Picsart Flow: make the kit in the app

Your Picsart plan includes Flow, its workflow builder. Flow can't import a workflow from a file, so build these two once (about 10 minutes) and reuse them. Running them uses your plan, not the API credits.

Flow 1: Lodestone texture

  1. On the web, open picsart.com/create/workflows and choose Create new workflow. Name it "Lodestone texture".
  2. Input step: Text prompt.
  3. Generate step: pick Flux 3 Image (or Flux 3 Image Klein if that's what your plan lists). Set aspect ratio 1:1, 1 image, highest resolution offered. Connect Input to Generate.
  4. Output step: PNG, best quality. Connect Generate to Output. Save.

Flow 2: Lodestone prop

  1. Duplicate Flow 1 and name it "Lodestone prop".
  2. Keep the Generate step, but set the aspect ratio per prop (shown on each prompt below: 2:3 for tall things, 1:1 for the rest).
  3. Add a Remove background step between Generate and Output. Make Output keep transparency (PNG).
  4. If Flow lets a step have two outputs, also save the Generate image before the background is removed. The script uses both.

Run it and hand it over

  1. Paste one prompt below, run, and keep the best of a few tries.
  2. Save it as <name>_raw.png (props: the image with the cream background) in C:\Users\Brand\Claude\lodestone-art\picsart\kit\raw\, replacing the old one. For props, save the background-removed image as <name>_cut.png in the same folder.
  3. Tell me "new art in". I'll run tools/art/picsart_kit.py --process-only on your PC (free, no API): it makes textures seamless, matches each to its palette colour, trims props to their base point and drops everything into the game.

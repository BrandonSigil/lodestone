Part I

What Lodestone is

Six days of building. A playable loop from an empty cottage to a boss fight, a full map maker inside the game, and roughly 270 design calls you've made along the way.

The game in one breath

A tactics RPG where the road wears you down and home builds you back up

You wake up alone in a village that looks fifty years abandoned. You take one weapon from the smithy, rescue a dog, and lift a glowing stone from a statue in the square. Then you walk out the gate. Every outing is a push into hand-made maps full of height, cliffs and water, with Fire Emblem-style fights where position is everything. When you fall, the Lodestone carries the whole party home with everything they earned. Home is where you rest, cook, rebuild the town and pick who goes next. Then you go again, a little further.

Tactics first

Grid fights with height, flanks, guard zones and team-ups. Melee focused, readable from one fixed camera. [USER]

The road costs

HP, abilities and items carry between fights. You arrive at the boss battered. [USER]

Home heals

Falling is free. The village restores, the party rests, and the next run starts stronger. [USER]

Earth to Alien

Familiar fantasy first. Past the Wall, things grow stranger until nothing is known. [USER]

Systems today

What every system does right now

Each block is a system that runs on main, its rules in short, and a screenshot from this afternoon's build. Numbers live in game/data/ and are tunable without code.

Combat BUILT

  • Fire Emblem phases: you move every piece, then every foe moves. [USER]
  • Hits always land. Damage wobbles by ±1, armor (0 to 3) comes off, crits are 10% and double; a flank adds 20% crit. High ground adds up to +2 damage.
  • Cover blocks sight and is half cover against ranged (−1). The danger zone is the only warning, except a boss's big move, which marks its tiles a phase ahead.
  • Guard zones: foes next to a guard must hit it or take a free hit to leave.
  • Two teammates next to the same foe team up: a helping strike adds damage.
  • Downed pieces bleed out over 3 phases; AID brings them back; a foe can spend its action to knock one out. Nobody dies for good.
  • Each job pays its own way: mana (mages), rage (knights), charges (dog, rogue, archer).
  • Fights win by goals: defeat, beat the leader, hold for N rounds, reach the exit, protect. TALK appears when you know something.
  • Clutch tools: Fetch (the dog carries a friend), COMMAND (sword), SHADOW SWAP (daggers), OVERWATCH (bow), WARD (book).
  • Scale C: fight areas of 21 × 18 with move and reach ×1.25 (×1.5 for fast or long-range pieces). 30 Oct

Jobs and growth BUILT level-up rework designed

  • The weapon is the job: sword (knight), daggers (rogue), bow (archer), and a hidden book on a shelf for the mage. Swap at the smithy rack at home; each job keeps its own level. [USER]
  • Stats roll from each character's growth odds. A tank mostly gains HP or armor, never magic.
  • New on 31 Oct: odd levels give a stat bump; even levels let you learn a new ability or upgrade one. Leveling pops up as an event after the fight. [USER]
  • Ten class archetypes approved with full kits: bulwark, reaver, shade, sharpshooter, tracker, ember, moon, mender, chanter, tinker. Evolved classes upgrade a single skill. [USER]
  • Party of four. The party only changes at home, at the gate's EDIT PARTY.

The pack and loot BUILT

  • One 4 × 3 party pack of shaped items you can rotate, Diablo style. Meat stacks 10 a cell, potions 3. [USER]
  • Each member has a 2 × 1 belt; in a fight a piece uses only its own belt, and using it is its action.
  • Pack full on the map: throw something away, or fill the dog's 2 × 2 saddlebag and send him home.
  • Containers keep their shapes (barrel 2 × 2, chest 2 × 4). Chests can be locked, trapped, or both. After a fight, loot lands in one 4 × 4 grid.
  • Coming home, materials bank themselves; gear stays packed.

Morale, food and rest 31 Oct rules drafted, not built

  • Morale is per character, 0 to 100, and it persists after a run. Falls cost it; a fall nobody picks up costs more. Low morale weakens a character, Banner Saga style.
  • No hunger. Food is healing. Simple food heals; camping restores some morale; good food does both.
  • The village is the main way back: sleep there, or leave low-morale characters home to rest. Your newest note: each journey day away gives characters at home some morale.
  • Drafted bands: Heartened 80+ (+1 on checks), Steady, Shaken (−1 damage), Low (−1 move too), Broken 0–19 (−2 damage, −1 move, −1 armor).
  • Camping happens only on the world map; potions and food carry you between fights on explore maps. 30 Oct

The village and one more run BUILT

  • Trips bring home materials. The village restores the buildings already standing, step by step. Each step asks for specific items and finishes when you come back from your next trip. [USER]
  • Ladders today: smithy, inn kitchen, and weapon upgrades for sword, daggers, bow and book. Settlers move in by placeholder events.
  • Goals stay in view three ways, like Stardew's community center: at the spot, in a global list, and pinned in the frame.
  • Meals are prepared at home from the chef's recipes (stew, roast, feast) and cooked at camp on the road.
  • Road encounters give random blessings and setbacks for the trip; hand-made maps give lasting rewards: rare materials, keys and shortcuts, stat items.
  • A trip runs 30 to 45 minutes; save and quit anywhere outside a fight.

The dog and bonds BUILT

  • Doggo fights on the grid: BITE slows, BARK taunts, Fetch carries a friend to safety. Good Boy: the friend next to him heals 1 HP each turn, and party morale losses shrink while he's along. 31 Oct
  • He's a weak fighter but a strong explorer: sniffing out treasure and rare food is his real value (sniff not built yet).
  • Every pair in the party has a bond. Finishing fights together, helping each other up and teaming up raise it. Levels C, B, A give +1 team-up damage each and open a talk at home.

Exploration, overworld and bosses BUILT

  • Only the leader walks the map. When a fight starts, the others deploy in a ring around the leader; faster pieces start further out. [USER]
  • "!" interactables open choices gated by who's in the party, with d20 checks for talking, sneaking and smashing.
  • The overworld is a console-style node map. Cleared places show as done and reveal the next. Encounters: fights, choice events, items, abandoned homes. Hunt, fish (needs a rod) and forage are dice rolls for now.
  • Elite groups are badged, avoidable and stay beaten. Foes are set by depth.
  • The Lich King: undead (fire +2, arrows −2), raises ice pillars that chill anyone nearby, and his card shows his HP and moves after your first try. Sneaking past him still completes the region.

Part II · from the systems and gameplay designer

What Lodestone can be

Everything below is a proposal [AI]. It is built only from systems you've already chosen, wired so each one feeds another. Nothing here writes story; it builds the machine your story rides on.

The thesis

There and Back Again: three clocks

You named the loop yourself: There and Back Again. The road takes, home gives back. The trick that makes it addictive is that the road takes three different things, and home refills each one at a different speed. That gap is where every decision lives.

The body clock

HP, mana, charges, belt items. Spent fast, every fight. Refilled by potions, food, one camp a trip, and fully by going home.

resets: every trip

The spirit clock

Morale, per character. Spent slowly, by falls and hard events. Refilled only slowly: good food, a camp, nights at home, time on the bench.

resets: never by itself

The world clock

Levels, bonds, the village, what you know. Only ever grows. Falling never takes it away.

resets: never

Why this works. Falling is free for the body and the world, as you decided. But it dents the spirit of whoever fell. So a reckless run doesn't cost loot, it costs the next run's best fighter. That pushes you to rotate the roster, which makes every companion matter, which makes recruiting them exciting, which makes the next region worth the risk. One rule, five systems pulled taut.

Ten pitches · all [AI]

Ways to make every system pull on another

Each pitch names the systems it ties together, why it hooks a player, and the one call it needs from you. They're ordered by how much they lift the whole game for the work they cost.

A

The bench is a bed

Roster rotation as the main strategy between runs.

How it works
  • Characters left home gain morale per journey day (your 31 Oct note). A long outing heals the bench.
  • While resting, a benched character also works their settler-style trade for the village: the hunter stocks the larder, the tinker trims a restore step's cost, the chanter runs the inn's talk slot.
  • The gate's EDIT PARTY shows each character's morale band beside their portrait, so the choice reads at a glance.
Why it hooks
  • Every run starts with a real puzzle: your best four are not always your freshest four.
  • Recruiting a new companion is suddenly valuable even if they're weaker; they're fresh legs.
  • Darkest Dungeon's hamlet tension, without its cruelty: nobody dies, they just need a rest.
Your call: should a resting companion work a trade at home, or only rest?
B

Morale you can spend

Make the top of the bar a resource, not just the bottom a penalty.

How it works
  • A Heartened character (80+) may spend 10 morale once a fight for a surge: +1 move, or a crit that can't miss, or standing up from bleeding out on their own.
  • The Banner Saga did this with willpower; here it comes out of the same bar that falls drain.
  • Spending drops them toward Steady, so a surge today is a slower recovery tomorrow.
Why it hooks
  • High morale feels like a gift you earned, not a number you protect.
  • It creates clutch moments in boss fights, the stories players retell.
  • It makes good food and nights at home exciting: they buy surges.
Your call: spendable morale, or keep morale as a pure penalty for now?
C

Knowledge is the loot that never drops

The Lodestone remembers what you learned, and what you know opens new options.

How it works
  • Today a boss's card stays after your first try. Extend it: foe tags seen, a chest's trap spotted, a shortcut found, a hostile survivor's weak spot overheard all go into the stone for good.
  • That knowledge is what unlocks TALK in fights ("only when you know something", your rule), new event options on "!" things, and recruit paths.
  • A Lodestone page lists it, grouped by region, with blanks where something is still unknown.
Why it hooks
  • A failed run still pays. "I didn't win, but now I know about the ladder behind him."
  • Outer Wilds and Hades both live on this: the player gets stronger, not just the character.
  • It's the natural home for your writing: every entry is a slot for your text.
Your call: does the Lodestone keep a visible list of what you've learned?

E

The kitchen chain

Minigames feed meals, meals feed morale, morale feeds fights.

How it works
  • Fishing, hunting and herb gathering become short skill games on the overworld (each a minute or less). A good catch gives a better ingredient, not just more of it.
  • Ingredients go home in the pack and become the chef's meals: stew, roast, feast, each a full pack slot you chose to carry.
  • Cooked at camp, a meal heals, lifts morale and gives a fight buff for a few fights. Better ingredients make better meals.
Why it hooks
  • The minigame always matters because its output lands in the next fight.
  • The pack becomes a real bet, the Stardew mine trip you described: carry a feast out, or leave room for loot.
  • It gives quiet travel time a purpose, the way pack-sorting does.
Your call: which minigame first? Fishing is the smallest to build and the rod already exists.
F

Push or bank

Each cleared place on an outing is a fork: go deeper for more, or go home with what you have.

How it works
  • After clearing a place, the overworld shows what the next one offers (a material, an elite, a lasting reward) and what you'll arrive with (HP, kit, morale bars).
  • GO HOME banks everything. Pushing on risks a fall, which also banks everything but dents morale for whoever goes down.
  • A few places only reward a party that arrives deep in an outing: an elite that only shows up on your third stop, say.
Why it hooks
  • Classic push-your-luck, the engine of Slay the Spire's elites and Hades's chamber doors.
  • Falling stays free in goods (your rule) but the cost is the people, so the choice is real.
Your call: are some rewards only reachable deep in an outing?

Earth to Alien, as a system

Past the Wall you don't know what anything is, so knowing becomes the game.

How it works
  • Alien finds arrive unidentified, as plain shapes in the pack. Eating one unknown is a gamble: a heal, a blessing, or a setback.
  • A character with the right kit (moon, tracker, mender) can name it on the road; a settler at home names it for good.
  • Once named, it's in the Lodestone's knowledge (pitch C) forever, and its recipe opens in the kitchen (pitch E).
  • The look's strangeness dial already in the map maker turns up region by region; foe tags gain new ones nobody has seen.
Why it hooks
  • It turns your visual twist into a mechanical one: the player feels as lost as the hero.
  • It rewards curiosity, and every unknown is a reason to go one more time.
Your call: should unknown food past the Wall be a gamble you can eat blind?
I

Bonds you can see on the board

Bonds already add team-up damage. Let them shape morale and movement too.

How it works
  • At bond B, a bonded partner next to a downed friend halves their morale loss.
  • At bond A, the pair gets one shared move per fight (a duo move like Unicorn Overlord's), designed per pair.
  • The party panel draws a thin line between bonded pieces that stand next to each other.
Why it hooks
  • Fire Emblem players come for the pairs. It makes "who stands next to whom" an emotional choice as well as a tactical one.
  • It's the payoff for your bond talks: the talk opens, the move unlocks.
Your call: duo moves at bond A, yes or not yet?
J

The homecoming screen

Every return ends on one page that shows everything you gained, then the next goal.

How it works
  • After a fall or GO HOME: materials banked, XP and levels, bond points, morale changes, new knowledge, and which restore step just finished.
  • It closes on the next watched goal and how close you are ("2 of 3 metal").
  • On a fall it also shows the boss card you just learned.
Why it hooks
  • Hades and Rogue Legacy make losing feel like progress with exactly this beat.
  • It's the moment a player decides "one more run". Showing the next goal makes that decision for them.
Your call: a homecoming summary after every return?

Part III · from the dev and art team

How we build maps worth a screenshot

The map builder, the systems developer and the art runner on the process: what we use today, the flow we propose for every map from now on, and the tools we still need to build. Proposals are [AI].

Your standing rules for maps

Never a box

"Do a check and go 'hmm is the entire map basically just a big box?' if yes, dont show it to me and work on it. Unique shapes, jut outs, dynamics, verticality!"Brandon, 30 Oct, crypt redesign

Shape

  • Big height differences, cliffs and falls
  • Roads that wind and climb
  • Irregular outlines, never flat squares

Scale

  • Things make spatial sense: a boathouse 3–4× the hero
  • Doors fit the hero
  • The village is quaint, built into a mountain side

Readability

  • One approved angle per map
  • Every shelf reachable, stairs that read as the path
  • Tall things go see-through round a piece; low props stay solid

What we build with now

The map maker (F8)

  • Three phases on a step bar: Shape (size, look, generator, raise and lower, tidy, pillars, ground materials, path, river, stairs, bridges, decks, area move), Decor (the asset drawers), Play (party start, foes, interactables).
  • Flat sketch mode (Tab): a top-down pixel plan to block shapes fast, then flip back to the painted view.
  • Greybox view with buildings as boxes, to judge height and space.
  • Bridges drawn between two landings; brush size 1–4 for paths, rivers, bridges and stairs.
  • Named placeholder blobs that turn into art the moment ph_<name>.png lands.
  • Notes on any thing: "make this door smashable" becomes a ticket for us.

God mode

The "!" on any map jumps into the maker centred on the party, and Back to play returns to the same cell.

Map checks

Build scripts check every walkable cell is reachable, every thing is usable, buildings sit flat and stairs read along their flight.

Fight sims

AI-vs-AI balance runs, the outing sim, and the E2E robot that plays the loop and fails on any engine error.

Shot tools

Scripts that drive any map, menu or fight into a state and save a picture. Every image in this document came from them.

Picsart pipeline

GPT image through the API, your flat forest panel as the only reference. A go card before each batch, a review card after.

The Studio desk

Your decisions as cards with screenshots. Every map review in this project went through it.

The map pipeline: eight stages from brief to postcard

Today maps are built and then reviewed as a whole, and your notes often come back at the shape stage ("so flat and square") after the dressing work is done. This flow puts your eye on the shape first, and checks the things you keep repeating by machine before you ever see a picture.

1

The brief

One card per map: what it's for (road fight, explore map, showcase, hub), its fight goals, target length, the elite and lasting reward it holds, and its postcard: the one view a player should screenshot. You approve the brief, not a picture.

Studio card · brief template (to build)
2

Flat sketch

Block the shape top-down in flat mode: districts, heights as shades, the route, choke points, where the postcard is seen from. Fast to change, cheap to throw away.

Flat sketch mode (built)
3

Greybox

Flip to 3D greybox. Sculpt the cliffs, stairs, bridges and decks. Buildings as boxes at their real footprints.

Shape phase, greybox view, area move, bridge tool (built)
4

Machine QA

Before anyone looks: every cell reachable, no stair to nowhere, a "box score" that fails a map whose outline is too square or its heights too flat, scale checks against the hero, enough deploy room at each fight.

Reachability (built in scripts) · box score, scale and deploy checks in the maker (to build)
5

Shape review

You see the greybox from the play angle and the postcard angle. Approve the shape, or send it back while it's cheap.

Studio card with greybox shots
6

Play pass

Place the party start, foe groups, "!" things, containers, elites, TALK fights. The robot walks it; the sim fights every group; the outing sim checks what the party arrives at the boss with.

Play phase, god mode, sims, E2E robot (built)
7

Dress

Tile sets, props, transitions, water, ambience. Every missing piece is a named blob, and the blobs roll up into one Picsart go card per map.

Decor phase, blobs (built) · blob-to-go-card queue (to build)
8

Postcard pass

Frame the reveal: the camera move as you arrive, ambience, a set piece in view. Shoot the postcard and the before and after. You stamp it and it ships.

Shot tools (built) · camera marks and reveal moves (to build)
Showcase moments

What makes a player stop and take a screenshot

Your brief asked for "frequent moments where a player pauses and takes a screenshot". From the maps you've approved and the games in the research deck, these are the ingredients. A showcase map should have at least three.

The reveal

You climb a stair, the camera eases back, and the whole place opens below you. The crypt's courtyard-bridge-doors approach is built for this.

Vertical drama

A waterfall beside a stair, a bridge you walk under and later over, a ledge that overlooks the square.

A living frame

Swaying trees, falling snow and leaves, moving water. All approved, all built.

The arena changes

The Lich's ice pillars rising. A boss whose phases at 2/3 and 1/3 could flood a terrace or drop a bridge.

The choice in view

Your lava monster with a ladder behind him: two ways to win, both visible from the start.

Quiet places

The nooks in the village: the clearing at the head of the falls, the glade behind the hall. Rewards for looking.

What would make building fast and good

1

The QA panel in the maker

One button that runs reachability, the box score, stairs to nowhere, scale against the hero and deploy room, and paints the problems on the map. It catches what you keep repeating before a picture reaches you.

2

Fight and encounter editor

Set a group's goal (hold, exit, protect, leader), its TALK unlock, its elite badge and reward in the Play phase, then run the sim on that one fight from the maker.

3

Map triggers

Zones and things that fire when a piece steps in: a camera move, a door opening, water rising, a placeholder line for your text. The backbone of set pieces and story beats alike.

4

Camera marks and the postcard view

Mark a reveal point and a framing on the map; the game eases the camera there on arrival, and the shot tool saves the postcard on demand.

5

Prefabs and district kits

Save a built piece (a home with its landing, a stair-and-terrace, a bridge with railings) and stamp it elsewhere, so a new village or ruin starts from proven pieces.

6

Overworld editor

Nodes, forks that rejoin, encounters per leg, and which place a cleared place reveals. Today the overworld is in code.

7

The blob-to-art queue

Every named blob across all maps listed with its footprint, scale and note, grouped into a ready Picsart go card with the right references.

8

New biome kits

The prairie and the north-west need tile sets, props and a look; past the Wall needs the first alien kit and a strangeness ramp.

9

Light and sound per map

Time of day tint, a fog or glow layer, and ambient sound zones (water, wind, a forge) painted like a zone.
